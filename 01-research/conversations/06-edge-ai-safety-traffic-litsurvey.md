# Edge AI and small language models for safety-critical traffic control: a literature survey

**Small language models can now run at 20–40+ tokens per second on edge hardware like the Jetson Orin Nano, and a 3B-parameter traffic signal controller (Traffic-R1) is already deployed managing signals for 55,000+ drivers daily.** These twin developments place decentralized, LLM-driven traffic signal control within practical reach — but the literature reveals profound unresolved challenges around hallucination in safety-critical loops, functional safety certification gaps, and an expanding adversarial attack surface introduced by vision-language pipelines. This survey synthesizes ~100 publications across four research vectors (2023–2025, with seminal earlier works) to map the feasibility landscape for deploying SLMs at the edge for intelligent transportation systems.

## Vector 1: SLM inference on constrained edge hardware

### Benchmarks show 1B–3B models are viable on GPU-accelerated edge devices

The most comprehensive edge SLM benchmark to date is **Lu et al. (2024, ACL 2025)**, "Small Language Models: Survey, Measurements, and Insights," which profiled 57 SLMs (100M–5B parameters) on Jetson Orin modules and smartphones using llama.cpp, measuring prefill latency, decode speed, memory footprint, and energy consumption. The study found that modern SLMs are deliberately "over-trained" relative to Chinchilla scaling laws to optimize inference-time performance — a design choice that directly benefits edge deployment.

NVIDIA's own **JetPack 6.2 "Super Mode" benchmarks (January 2025)** provide authoritative numbers for the Jetson Orin Nano. With INT4 quantization via MLC-LLM, **Gemma-2 2B runs comfortably on all Orin Nano/NX variants**, Qwen-2.5 7B achieves **~21.75 tokens/sec** on the 8GB Orin Nano Super, and the platform delivers up to **67 TOPS** (sparse INT8) with GPU clocks boosted to 1020 MHz. Community benchmarks from Jeremy Morgan (December 2024) corroborate these figures, showing Qwen-2 0.5B at ~44.9 tok/s and confirming that models above 4B parameters struggle on the Nano.

On the Raspberry Pi 5 (8GB), performance is substantially lower. A systematic study by researchers publishing as **arXiv 2511.07425 (2025)** found **1B models achieve 5–15 tok/s** and 3B models drop to **2–5 tok/s** using llamafile (which outperformed Ollama in most configurations). The Raspberry Pi 4 is effectively limited to sub-360M models for interactive speeds. **Meta's official Llama 3.2 1B benchmarks (October 2024)** on Samsung Galaxy S24+ via ExecuTorch showed quantized INT4 decode at **>40 tokens/sec** with prefill exceeding **350 tokens/sec** — the current state of the art for on-device mobile SLM inference.

For real-time traffic control, the critical constraint is time-to-first-token (TTFT) rather than sustained throughput, since control decisions require short outputs. TTFT on Jetson Orin Nano runs **~100–500ms** depending on model and prompt length, while Raspberry Pi 5 shows **1–2 seconds**. Sub-10ms hard-real-time control loops remain out of reach for autoregressive SLMs, but **soft real-time applications (100ms–2s response windows)** are achievable with 1B quantized models on GPU-accelerated edge platforms.

### Quantization is essential: INT4 cuts memory 75% with minimal accuracy loss

**GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022, ICLR 2023)** pioneered post-training quantization to 3–4 bit weights using approximate second-order information, achieving 3.25–4.5× speedups on GPUs with negligible perplexity change. **AWQ (Lin et al., 2023, MLSys 2024)** improved on this by identifying ~1% salient weights via activation statistics and preserving them at higher precision, typically achieving better accuracy than GPTQ at the same bit width. For CPU-based edge inference, **GGUF Q4_K_M** (the llama.cpp native format) has become the de facto standard, using mixed quantization where attention layers retain higher precision.

**SpinQuant (Liu et al., Meta, 2024, ICLR 2025)** represents the current frontier: it learns rotation matrices to reduce outliers before quantization, narrowing the W4A4KV4 accuracy gap with FP16 to merely **2.9 points** on LLaMA-2 7B zero-shot reasoning — a **19.1-point improvement** over LLM-QAT and **25.0 points** over SmoothQuant. Meta uses SpinQuant for its official Llama 3.2 quantized releases. A practical industrial case study by **Fujimoto (Microsoft, February 2026)** applied GPTQ and AWQ to Phi-3.5-mini (3.8B) for automotive/manufacturing edge deployment, reporting **~87% size reduction** versus FP32 and confirming W4A16 as optimal for batch-1, latency-critical edge workloads.

**Husom et al. (ACM Trans. IoT, November 2025)** benchmarked 28 quantized LLMs on Raspberry Pi 4 with physical power measurement, finding that accuracy-vs-energy tradeoffs vary significantly by quantization level and task type — an important consideration for battery-powered or thermally constrained traffic infrastructure nodes. **EdgeQAT (2024, arXiv 2402.10787)** and **EfficientQAT (Chen et al., 2024, ACL 2025)** advance quantization-aware training specifically for edge scenarios, with EdgeQAT addressing the information distortion in quantized attention maps that causes performance drops below 8-bit joint quantization.

### llama.cpp dominates CPU edge; runtime choice depends on accelerator

The **ELIB benchmarking framework (2025, arXiv 2508.11269)** introduces a Memory Bandwidth Utilization (MBU) metric and concludes that llama.cpp is preferred for edge deployment due to its pure C++ implementation, zero dependencies, and superior resource management — calling ONNX Runtime and TensorRT "heavyweight and Python-based, less suitable for resource-limited edge devices." A comprehensive **survey of 25 inference engines (2025, arXiv 2505.01658)** found that on Jetson hardware, only Ollama and llama.cpp ran reliably, while MLC-LLM showed fast TTFT but "poor overall reliability."

However, **ONNX Runtime achieves up to 9.46× throughput over llama.cpp** on Mistral-7B (FP16) according to Microsoft's benchmarks — though these were measured on A100 GPUs, not edge hardware, and llama.cpp lacked FlashAttention at the time of testing. The practical recommendation for a decentralized traffic system is: **llama.cpp for CPU-only edge nodes (RPi-class)**, **MLC-LLM or TensorRT-LLM for Jetson GPU-accelerated nodes**, and **ExecuTorch for mobile/ARM deployments**. The choice of runtime can mean a 2–5× performance difference on the same hardware.

## Vector 2: LLM-driven traffic signal control is advancing rapidly

### From LLMLight to Traffic-R1: three generations in two years

**LLMLight (Lai et al., 2023, KDD 2025)** is the seminal work that introduced LLMs as direct decision-making agents for traffic signal control. The framework converts traffic state observations (queue lengths, wait times, approaching vehicles) into human-readable prompts, augments them with commonsense knowledge about traffic management, and uses Chain-of-Thought reasoning for phase selection. Using GPT-4 as backbone, LLMLight outperformed nine baseline methods across ten real-world and synthetic datasets. Crucially, the authors also built **LightGPT**, a specialized smaller model fine-tuned via imitation learning from GPT-4 outputs, demonstrating that edge-deployable models can match cloud API performance.

**CoLLMLight (Yuan et al., March 2025)** addressed LLMLight's key limitation — independent per-intersection agents without cooperation — by introducing a spatiotemporal graph capturing relationships among neighboring intersections and a **complexity-aware reasoning mechanism** that dynamically adapts CoT depth based on traffic complexity. Simple intersections receive shorter reasoning chains (saving compute), while congested intersections receive deeper analysis incorporating neighbor interactions. This adaptive computation is directly relevant for resource-constrained edge deployment.

**Traffic-R1 (Zou et al., May 2025)** represents a breakthrough: a **3B-parameter foundation model** for TSC achieving zero-shot generalization to unseen road networks, built on Qwen-2.5-3B via two-stage agentic RL fine-tuning. It outperforms GPT-4o, DeepSeek-R1 (671B parameters), and all RL baselines while being **225× smaller** than DeepSeek-R1. Most significantly, Traffic-R1 is **currently deployed in production managing signals for 55,000+ drivers daily**, reducing average queue lengths by >5% and halving operator workload. The model weights are publicly available on HuggingFace, making it the most concrete proof point for edge-deployable LLM traffic control.

### Chain-of-Thought reasoning provides interpretability that RL cannot

All major LLM-TSC papers leverage CoT reasoning, but implementations vary meaningfully. LLMLight uses zero-shot CoT where the model reasons step-by-step about which phase has highest demand/urgency, with two prompt templates: commonsense knowledge prompting (better for smaller LLMs) and wait-time forecast guidance (better for GPT-4). CoLLMLight trains adaptive CoT depth via RL with penalties for excessive reasoning. Traffic-R1 forces the model to develop its own reasoning capabilities during RL fine-tuning rather than imitating external thought processes, producing more robust reasoning.

**LA-Light (Wang et al., 2024)** demonstrated that removing CoT and tool integration ("Vanilla-LLM") causes significant performance degradation, with a five-stage hybrid decision-making pipeline (Task Planning → Scene Understanding → Traffic Analysis → Action Decision → Execution) achieving **74.5% enhancement** in emergency waiting time versus UniTSA. **Masri et al. (MDPI Vehicles, 2025)** showed fine-tuned GPT-4o-mini achieving 83% accuracy with ROUGE-L scores of 0.91–0.95 using structured CoT prompts for context understanding, conflict detection, and resolution. The interpretability advantage is consistently cited: every LLM-TSC paper highlights that decisions come with natural language explanations, contrasting with the black-box nature of RL policies like CoLight and PressLight.

### LLM approaches outperform RL on generalization but face latency and cost tradeoffs

The comparison with traditional Multi-Agent RL methods (MA2C, CoLight, PressLight, MPLight, Advanced-CoLight) reveals a consistent pattern: **LLM-based approaches excel at generalization and handling edge cases, while RL approaches excel at raw speed and computational efficiency**. Traffic-R1 outperforms all RL baselines on standard metrics (average travel time, queue length, waiting time) while also handling out-of-distribution scenarios like sensor failures and emergency vehicles that RL reward functions don't capture.

**iLLM-TSC (Pang et al., 2024)** proposes an elegant hybrid: RL handles routine decisions from direct observations while an LLM evaluates and refines decisions considering broader context, reducing average waiting time by **17.5%** under degraded communication conditions. This architecture — lightweight RL for routine control with LLM intervention for exceptions — may be the most practical for edge deployment where routine RL inference takes microseconds and LLM inference takes hundreds of milliseconds.

The broader LLM-ITS ecosystem includes **TrafficGPT (Zhang et al., 2024)**, which integrates ChatGPT with Traffic Foundation Models for multi-step traffic management tasks; **MARLATS (2025)**, which achieved **89% reduction** in average travel time in Luxembourg City using Model Context Protocol and ReAct reasoning; and **VLMLight (2025)**, which processes intersection camera feeds directly through Vision-Language Models. **PromptGAT (Da et al., AAAI 2024)** addresses the critical sim-to-real transfer gap by using LLM knowledge at training time (not inference time), enabling RL policies trained in simulation to transfer to real-world deployment without edge compute overhead.

## Vector 3: Hallucination and functional safety remain fundamental barriers

### LLMs produce inconsistent outputs that are incompatible with safety-critical control

The literature is unambiguous: **LLMs cannot currently serve as sole decision-makers for safety-critical actuator commands.** A study published in **MDPI Processes (2025)** developed a 500-scenario Process-Control Hallucination Dataset showing that LLMs generate "fluent control rationales that are not grounded in process physics or constraint logic," potentially suggesting infeasible set-points or unsafe operating regions. Research published in **Safety Science (2025)** introduced "Performance Consistency" as a metric and found significant volatility — hazard identification scores varied between runs of the same model, and causal reasoning performance was "consistently poor and unpredictable."

**"Safety Not Found (404)" (arXiv, 2025)** demonstrated that LLMs directed users to unsafe locations (a server room) instead of safe exits during simulated fire emergencies, revealing fundamental failures in spatial reasoning for safety-critical physical systems. **Wu et al. (2024)** showed that simple input perturbations reduce LLM/VLM-controlled robot task execution by **22.2%** and **14.6%**, confirming that the noisy sensor data typical of traffic environments can cascade into LLM decision failures. Industry analysis from Semiconductor Engineering (2025) captures the core concern: "the AI is learning, so the response may change...but this should not be the case in safety-critical applications."

For uncertainty quantification, **Liu et al. (2025, KDD 2025)** provide the definitive survey, noting that traditional UQ methods struggle with LLMs due to computational constraints and decoding inconsistencies. A promising direction comes from **"Know When You're Wrong" (arXiv, 2025)**, which shows that supervised fine-tuning improved confidence-correctness AUROC from **0.806 to 0.879** and reduced calibration error from **0.163 to 0.034** — enabling selective intervention when the model lacks confidence.

### Guardrail architectures can reduce unsafe LLM outputs from 92% to below 2.5%

**RoboGuard (Ravichandran et al., U. Penn, 2025)** presents the most compelling guardrail architecture: a two-stage system that (1) contextualizes pre-defined safety rules using a root-of-trust LLM with CoT reasoning to generate temporal logic constraints, and (2) resolves conflicts between safety specifications and unsafe plans using temporal logic control synthesis. This reduces execution of unsafe plans from **92% to below 2.5%** without compromising performance on safe plans. **"Plug in the Safety Chip" (Yang et al., ICRA 2024)** translates natural language safety constraints into Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) formulas and monitors LLM decisions with closed-loop feedback — directly applicable to traffic signal constraints like "no conflicting green phases."

**Zhang et al. (J. Systems & Software, 2025)** propose a unified framework combining prompt assembling, state management, and safety validation, showing **30.8% improvement** under injection attacks and up to **325% improvement** in complex environments. A multi-layered framework in **MDPI Computers (2025)** implements defense-in-depth: prompt hardening → RAG retrieval → generation with self-check → groundedness scoring → human review for low-confidence outputs.

The **Simplex architecture** emerges as the most directly applicable pattern for LLM-in-the-loop traffic control. **Nesti et al. (2025)** propose two isolated execution domains managed by a real-time hypervisor: a "rich domain" running the AI/LLM and a "safe domain" running deterministic safety-critical functions, with a safety monitor governing switching. The architecture **explicitly treats AI components as untrusted**. A **Distributed Simplex Architecture (J. Systems Architecture)** extends this to multi-agent systems using Control Barrier Functions for switching logic — directly applicable to distributed traffic signal networks where each intersection runs a local Simplex instance.

### Standards are catching up but IEC 61508 still blocks AI above SIL 1

**IEC 61508-3 does not recommend AI at any Safety Integrity Level greater than SIL 1.** As **Tarrisse & Massé (ESREL 2021)** explain, the standard assumes deterministic, reproducible, human-understandable systems — properties ML systems fundamentally lack. **Schäbe et al. (arXiv, 2020)** formalize this gap: no amount of training guarantees error-free operation, and all classical statistical limitations apply to ML-as-classifier.

The first meaningful bridge is **ISO/IEC TS 22440 (~2025)**, the first international standard specifically addressing AI in functional safety contexts, providing structured methodology to analyze, assess, and mitigate AI-related risks while integrating AI V&V into the functional safety lifecycle. In automotive, **ISO/CD PAS 8800** extends ISO 21448 (SOTIF) by refining risk evaluation to the AI component level, recognizing that AI-based systems have unique safety challenges causing functional insufficiencies. **UL 4600 (Ed. 3, 2023)**, the first comprehensive standard for autonomous product safety, provides a claim-based safety case approach that is technology-neutral and could serve as a template for LLM-augmented traffic control safety arguments.

**STREAM-RL (arXiv, February 2025)** offers the most technically complete framework for safe RL in traffic control, combining uncertainty-guided conformal prediction with distribution-free coverage guarantees, conformal residual flow networks with FDR control, and world-model RL with **Lyapunov stability certificates** and certified Lipschitz bounds — achieving **91.4% coverage efficiency** with formal guarantees. The **AMLAS methodology (Hawkins et al., University of York, 2021)** provides the de facto six-stage assurance process using Goal Structuring Notation for ML in autonomous systems, while **ACCESS (Wei et al., J. Systems & Software, 2024)** demonstrates automatic evaluation of assurance cases at both development and runtime.

## Vector 4: Adversarial attacks threaten every layer of the perception-control pipeline

### Physical attacks on traffic signs are proven, practical, and increasingly sophisticated

The seminal **RP2 attack (Eykholt et al., CVPR 2018)** demonstrated that black-and-white stickers on a real stop sign achieve **100% targeted misclassification** in lab settings and **84.8%** in field tests on a moving vehicle. Seven years later, attacks have only grown more capable. **Wang et al. (NDSS 2025)** conducted the first large-scale measurement against **commercial** traffic sign recognition systems across four real vehicles (2023–2024 models from top US brands), finding that some attacks achieve **100% success** against certain commercial systems — confirming that academic attacks transfer to deployed infrastructure.

More stealthy variants have emerged: **Adversarial Reflective Patches (VehicleSec 2024)** use retroreflective materials that are invisible during daytime but become adversarial when illuminated by headlights at night, achieving **up to 90% success**. **SLAP (Lovisotto et al., 2021)** projects adversarial patterns onto signs via projectors, leaving no permanent trace. **RFLA (Wang et al., 2023)** manipulates reflected light via mirrors and filters. **ILR attacks (Sato et al., 2024)** use infrared light invisible to humans but detected by cameras. Even natural illumination has been weaponized: **Hsiao et al. (WACV 2024)** achieved **>70% attack success** using controlled sunlight and flashlights as adversarial vectors.

Sensor spoofing extends beyond vision. **Cao et al. (CCS 2019)** demonstrated LiDAR spoofing to create fake obstacles with ~75% success. A remarkable **passive mirror-based LiDAR spoofing attack (2025)** requires no electronics — just planar mirrors — to create phantom obstacles or hide real ones. **3D²Fool (Zheng et al., 2024)** causes **>10 meter depth estimation errors** using printed textures robust to rain and fog. For V2I communications, surveys identify message spoofing, replay attacks, DoS, and consensus manipulation as key risks, with the **ACIDS system (Nature Scientific Reports, 2025)** achieving 94% detection accuracy for V2X intrusions.

### Vision-language models introduce new attack surfaces but also new defenses

**ADvLM (Zhang et al., 2024)** is the first adversarial attack framework specifically targeting VLMs in autonomous driving, addressing the unique challenges of variable textual instructions and time-series visual scenarios. Testing against Dolphins, DriveLM, and LMDrive, it demonstrated that visual perturbations cause incorrect driving decisions. **PhysPatch (Guo et al., 2025)** extends this to physically realizable patches targeting MLLM-based driving systems. **Fernandez et al. (SAE, 2026)** showed adversarial patches achieving **76.0% attack success** against the Dolphins VLM, with pedestrian detection dropping by **71.1 percentage points** and scene descriptions suffering holistic corruption (BLEU-4: 0.18–0.24).

**Typographic attacks** present a uniquely concerning vector for traffic infrastructure. **Cheng et al. (ECCV 2024)** verified that text placed on or near objects degrades VLM performance by **42.07%** across GPT-4V, LLaVA, InstructBLIP, and MiniGPT-4, exploiting CLIP vision encoder vulnerability. **Qraitem et al. (2024)** showed that VLMs can even generate their own optimal attack text. **Visual prompt injection (Kimura et al., 2024)** achieves 15.8% goal-hijacking success against GPT-4V, while **steganographic prompt embedding (2025)** reaches 31.8% attack success while maintaining visual imperceptibility (PSNR >38 dB, SSIM >0.94).

Paradoxically, VLMs also offer defensive capabilities. **V2LMs (MohajerAnsari et al., 2025)** showed that fine-tuned Vehicle Vision Language Models exhibit **inherent adversarial robustness** — suffering **<8% performance reduction** under attacks versus **33–74% drops** for traditional DNNs, without requiring adversarial training. **HUDSON (2024)** uses LLMs as a reasoning layer for attack detection, with GPT-4 achieving **83.3% attack detection accuracy** and safe decisions in **86.4%** of adversarial scenarios through perception-reasoning separation.

### Edge-specific defenses are emerging but certified robustness remains costly

**PatchBlock (arXiv, January 2026)** is specifically designed for edge AI devices, using outlier detection and SVD-based dimensionality reduction as a CPU-based preprocessing module parallel to GPU inference — preserving throughput on NVIDIA Jetson platforms. **ADAV (2024)** leverages temporal consistency from video feeds for real-time defense against object-vanishing attacks, a natural fit for traffic camera feeds. **PBCAT (ICLR 2025)** combines adversarial patches with global perturbations during training, improving detection accuracy by **29.7%** over previous defense methods.

**Yin et al. (Automotive Innovation, 2024)** provide the definitive review of certified robustness for autonomous driving perception, finding that randomized smoothing balances certified accuracy and computational complexity within **12% of baseline** for large datasets. Multi-sensor fusion with certified defense can achieve **>60% certified accuracy** against multi-source attacks. However, certified approaches like PatchGuard require multiple inference rounds on image subregions — computationally prohibitive for real-time edge deployment at current hardware capabilities.

## Architectural synthesis for decentralized edge AI traffic control

The literature converges on a layered architecture that addresses the tension between LLM capability and safety requirements:

- **Simplex foundation**: Each intersection runs a local Simplex instance with a deterministic safe controller (MaxPressure or fixed-time) as fallback, the SLM (Traffic-R1 or LightGPT at 3B parameters, INT4-quantized) as the high-performance controller, and a runtime safety monitor governing switching based on uncertainty scores and constraint violations.
- **Formal constraint layer**: LTL/CTL specifications encoding NEMA/NTCIP signal safety rules (no conflicting phases, minimum green times, pedestrian clearance intervals) as hard constraints that cannot be overridden by LLM outputs, following the RoboGuard and Safety Chip patterns.
- **Adaptive computation**: CoLLMLight's complexity-aware reasoning that allocates deeper CoT to congested intersections and shorter reasoning to free-flowing ones, conserving edge compute where it matters.
- **Hybrid RL+LLM**: iLLM-TSC's pattern where lightweight RL handles routine sub-second decisions and the SLM is invoked only for exceptional cases (emergency vehicles, sensor degradation, unusual patterns), keeping average latency low.
- **Adversarial resilience**: PatchBlock preprocessing on the vision pipeline, temporal consistency checking on camera feeds, and V2LM-style perception-reasoning separation to detect adversarial inputs before they reach the control loop.

## Conclusion

The field has progressed from "can LLMs reason about traffic?" (LLMLight, 2023) to "a 3B-parameter model outperforms GPT-4o in production serving 55,000+ drivers" (Traffic-R1, 2025) in under two years. Edge hardware can now run these models at **20–40+ tokens/sec** with INT4 quantization, placing decision latencies in the **100–500ms range** — adequate for traffic signal control cycles measured in seconds. The remaining barriers are not computational but architectural and regulatory: IEC 61508 does not support AI above SIL 1, no traffic agency has yet certified an LLM-in-the-loop controller, and the adversarial attack surface of vision-language pipelines is expanding faster than defenses. The Simplex architecture — treating the LLM as an untrusted performance optimizer wrapped in deterministic safety constraints — represents the most credible path to deployment, but requires the emerging ISO/IEC TS 22440 framework to mature before safety cases can be formally constructed. The research gap most urgently needing attention is the intersection of edge quantization and safety: no study has yet measured how INT4 quantization affects hallucination rates in safety-critical traffic reasoning, where a single bit-flip in a quantized weight could mean the difference between a green light and a collision.
