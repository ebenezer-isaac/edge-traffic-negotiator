# Speculative design toolkit for The Edge Negotiator

**Decentralized autonomous traffic systems sit at the intersection of systems thinking, edge AI, and speculative design — and Dunne & Raby's Future Cones framework offers the ideal scaffold for imagining how a Code of Conduct for such a system might function across probable, plausible, possible, and preferable futures.** This report synthesizes the theoretical frameworks, technical realities, narrative techniques, and academic writing strategies needed to produce 5–6 vivid speculative scenarios for UCL COMP0184 coursework. It draws together Meadows' leverage points, Sterling's diegetic prototypes, real-world edge AI deployments, and the rhetorical craft of design fiction into a single actionable resource.

## The Future Cones: from probable to preposterous

The Futures Cone originated with Norman Henchey (1978), was first visualized by Hancock & Bezold (1994), expanded by Joseph Voros (2003, 2017), and popularized for designers by Dunne & Raby in *Speculative Everything* (2013, MIT Press). The cone's apex represents the present; it widens into the future as uncertainty grows. Four nested categories define the design space:

**Probable futures** occupy the narrowest cone — what is likely to happen based on current trends. Dunne & Raby note that "probably 90, no 95, no 99 percent of our attention" in conventional design goes here. For The Edge Negotiator, a probable scenario might depict incremental deployment of adaptive traffic signals with modest AI, governed by conventional municipal regulations. **Plausible futures** expand to what *could* happen, grounded in current understanding of how the world works. This is the territory of Shell-style scenario planning — credible extrapolations requiring no new physics. **Possible futures** stretch to what *might* happen if we acquired knowledge we don't yet possess, constrained only by the laws of physics. Dunne & Raby reference Michio Kaku's *Physics of the Impossible* as a boundary marker. **Preferable futures** cut across the probable and plausible zones as a normative overlay — what we *want* to happen. Critically, Dunne & Raby ask: "what does preferable mean, for whom, and who decides?"

Voros added two important extensions. The **preposterous** zone — futures deemed "ridiculous" or "impossible" — honors James Dator's Second Law: "any useful idea about the future should appear ridiculous." The boundary between preposterous and possible is the **Clarke-Dator Boundary**. Beyond all cones lies **fantasy**, which Dunne & Raby explicitly exclude: "Fantasy exists in its own world, with very few if any links to the world we live in."

For coursework, each scenario should be explicitly positioned within a cone and the rationale defended. A strong submission might include one scenario per cone (probable, plausible, possible, preferable) plus one at the preposterous boundary and one depicting an *unpreferable* future — giving six scenarios total. The accompanying analysis should explain why each scenario sits where it does, referencing the criteria: probable = trend-based data, plausible = current knowledge, possible = future knowledge, preferable = normative values.

### Dunne & Raby's projects as models

**United Micro Kingdoms** (2012–13) is the most instructive precedent for this coursework. England is divided into four "super-shires," each governed by a different ideology-technology pairing: Digitarians (neoliberal digital technocracy), Communo-Nuclearists (communism + nuclear energy on a 3km-long moving train), Anarcho-Evolutionists (anarchy + DIY bio-hacking), and Bioliberals (social democracy + biotechnology). Each kingdom has its own transport system, governance model, and material culture. The project operates in the plausible zone — extrapolating real ideologies and technologies to logical extremes. Its structure — **parallel governance models for the same territory** — maps directly onto writing parallel Codes of Conduct for The Edge Negotiator under different systemic assumptions.

**Designs for an Overpopulated Planet: Foragers** (2009) operates in the possible zone, imagining communities using synthetic biology to augment human digestion. The key design decision: "We avoided hyper-realism... It was very important that they clearly signaled their unreality so that viewers were aware they were looking at ideas, not products." This principle of **"slight strangeness"** — too weird and viewers dismiss it, not strange enough and it's absorbed into normality — is essential for calibrating scenario tone.

## Writing design fiction that feels inevitable

Three intellectual traditions converge on how to write compelling speculative scenarios, and each offers distinct techniques for the 200–500 word vignettes this coursework requires.

**Dunne & Raby** transform designed objects into "props for imagining alternative ways of thinking and behaving." Their method begins with "What if?" questions, grounds speculation in real science and emerging trends, creates design proposals (not products), and aims to provoke debate rather than present solutions. The narrative power comes from **world-building through objects** — each artifact implies an entire social, political, and economic system without explicitly describing it.

**Bruce Sterling** defined design fiction as "the deliberate use of diegetic prototypes to suspend disbelief about change." The concept of **diegetic prototypes** (from film scholar David Kirby) refers to designed objects that function fully within a fictional world, made legible through character interactions and narrative structure rather than exposition. Sterling's crucial insight: design fiction should feel **mundane, not spectacular**. The objects should exist in the gap between "buildable, profitable and desirable" and the "much larger space of potential objects" beyond those constraints. Bad design fiction says "wouldn't it be great if I could fly?" Good design fiction makes people think "that would be cool if it existed."

**Near Future Laboratory** (Julian Bleecker et al.) perfected the approach with the *TBD Catalog* (2014) — a SkyMall-style product catalog from a possible near future, complete with **166 products, 62 classifieds**, and advertisements. Their signature concept is **"the future mundane"**: "We depict a fantastic near future translated into its inevitably fraught, low-battery, poor reception, broken firmware, normal, ordinary, everyday sensibilities." Bleecker's strictest formulation: "Design Fictions are Material Artifacts. Not stories." The artifact should be "something a casual observer may mistake for a contemporary artefact, but which only reveals itself as a fiction on closer inspection." He calls this **"archaeology for the future"** — creating material evidence that implies entire worlds.

### Ten techniques for short speculative scenarios

For 200–500 word Code of Conduct scenarios, these techniques are most effective:

- **Start in the middle of action** — drop the reader into a moment, not exposition. A character encounters the Code in practice.
- **Use present tense** — creates immediacy and normalizes speculative elements. The technology already exists in the narrative.
- **Name specific products, interfaces, and model numbers** — "the Phi-4 Micro inference chip" or "Hyperledger Iroha v4.2 audit chain" grounds the scenario in material culture.
- **Include friction and failure** — things that don't work perfectly, bureaucratic frustrations, workarounds people have developed. The TBD Catalog's "Ear Freshener" for "confused ear syndrome" is a perfect example.
- **Embed the speculative element casually** — mention it the way you'd mention checking your phone, not as something extraordinary.
- **Focus on one character's experience** — a traffic engineer reviewing overnight negotiations, a cyclist whose route was deprioritized, a maintenance worker patching firmware.
- **Use mundane verbs** — checks, scrolls, swipes, waits, sighs. Not "activates the quantum neural mesh."
- **End with an unanswered question or ambiguity** — let the reader's imagination complete the world.
- **Show social context** — other people's reactions, normative expectations, things characters take for granted.
- **Never explain how the technology works** — just show what it does in practice. The explanation belongs in the Context and Explanation section.

A Code of Conduct is an ideal design fiction artifact because it is a **regulatory document audiences recognize**, it embeds values, power structures, and assumptions about acceptable behavior, and it implies an entire institutional ecosystem. Each clause of a fictional Code of Conduct is a micro-narrative about what a society has decided matters enough to regulate.

## Meadows' leverage points as scenario architecture

Donella Meadows' 12 leverage points, ranked from least to most effective intervention, provide the structural backbone for mapping Code of Conduct clauses to systemic impact. Each speculative scenario can target a different leverage level, creating escalating stakes across the cone categories.

The **lower-leverage points** (12–10) concern parameters, buffers, and physical structure. In traffic systems, these are speed limits, road capacity reserves, and network topology. A Code of Conduct operating at this level adjusts numerical thresholds — sensor polling rates, congestion pricing increments, queue buffer sizes. These are the **probable future** scenarios: familiar, incremental, minimally disruptive. Meadows observed that "probably 99 percent of our attention goes to parameters, but there's not a lot of leverage in them."

**Mid-leverage points** (9–6) address delays, feedback loops, and information flows. For The Edge Negotiator, **balancing feedback loops** like congestion pricing (higher traffic → higher toll → reduced demand) and **reinforcing loops** like induced demand (more roads → more drivers → more congestion → demand for more roads) are central dynamics. The critical concept of **information flows** — "who has access to what information" — maps directly to questions about whether edge nodes share negotiation data openly or encrypt it. A Code of Conduct mandating transparency of algorithmic decisions represents intervention at leverage point 6 (information flows), which Meadows considered often "cheaper and easier to change than physical structure" yet transformative.

The **high-leverage points** (5–1) are where the most powerful and speculative scenarios operate:

**Rules of the system** (point 5) — the protocols and algorithms governing agent interaction. Changing from fixed traffic schedules to adaptive self-organizing protocols fundamentally alters system behavior. A Code of Conduct that rewrites the negotiation protocol between edge nodes — from competitive auction to cooperative consensus — operates here. **Self-organization** (point 4) — the system's power to evolve its own structure. Meadows noted this is often "sacrificed for short-term efficiency." A scenario where The Edge Negotiator develops emergent behaviors its designers never intended illustrates this leverage point. **Goals** (point 3) — whether the system optimizes for vehicle throughput, human mobility, emissions reduction, or equity. **Paradigm** (point 2) — the shift from "centralized control is necessary for reliability" to "distributed intelligence is inherently more resilient." **Transcending paradigms** (point 1) — designing systems flexible enough to evolve their own goals, recognizing that no single optimization target is permanently "true."

### Feedback dynamics specific to traffic

Several feedback patterns are particularly relevant for scenarios. The **transit death spiral** is a reinforcing loop: lower ridership → reduced revenue → service cuts → further decline. **Braess's Paradox** shows that adding road capacity can worsen congestion — a counterintuitive result that makes excellent speculative material. **Self-organized traffic lights** (Helbing & Lämmer's research) convert "push operation" (precalculated plans) into "pull operation" (removing vehicles as fast as possible) through purely local interactions, producing emergent "green waves" that outperform centrally coordinated systems. **Empowerment-driven autonomous cars** (2025 research) use intrinsic motivation with only locally available information, achieving improved flow in fully decentralized models.

The concept of **cascading failures** provides dramatic material for darker scenarios. The 2003 Northeastern US blackout cascaded from a single local line failure to affect **55 million people**. Research in *Nature Communications* (2018) shows that even when networks appear robust, **rare but catastrophic cascades emerge through dynamical transients** lasting mere seconds. For The Edge Negotiator, a scenario where a single compromised edge node triggers a cascading negotiation failure across an entire urban grid illustrates both the vulnerability and the leverage point of system architecture.

The **edge of chaos** — the critical regime between order and disorder where complex adaptive systems achieve maximum innovation — is perhaps the richest concept for speculative scenarios. Stuart Kauffman's work shows that biological networks at criticality maximize both robustness and evolvability. London traffic data shows algebraically decaying spatio-temporal correlations akin to systems near a self-organized critical state — the first evidence for a large-scale human system reaching criticality. A Code of Conduct that tries to keep The Edge Negotiator operating at this productive boundary, neither rigidly ordered nor chaotically unstable, raises profound questions about governance of emergent systems.

## The technical substrate: what makes scenarios plausible

Grounding speculative scenarios in real technology is what separates plausible design fiction from fantasy. The current state of edge AI, blockchain, and autonomous traffic provides the factual foundation.

**NVIDIA's Jetson AGX Orin** delivers **275 TOPS of AI performance** at 15–60W, with ruggedized systems from manufacturers like Lanner (rated -40°C to 70°C) and SINTRONES (IP66-rated) already deployed in traffic environments. Edge devices process **30 frames per second** for real-time violation detection, reduce bandwidth by **80–84%** versus cloud systems, and operate independently — no single point of failure. Latency for safety-critical decisions must be **sub-10 milliseconds**, which cloud round-trips (800–2,400ms) cannot achieve.

**Microsoft Phi-3 Mini** (3.8 billion parameters, runs on **8GB RAM**) demonstrates that meaningful AI reasoning can execute on edge hardware. It scores 69% on MMLU, outperforms GPT-3.5 on reasoning benchmarks, and supports 128K-token context windows. Microsoft explicitly targets "car computers, traffic systems, smart sensors on factory floors" as deployment environments. For scenarios, the key detail: **SLMs enable offline, privacy-preserving decision-making** without cloud connectivity — each intersection node can reason autonomously.

**Hyperledger Iroha** (rewritten in Rust for Iroha 2) provides permissioned blockchain designed specifically for mobile and IoT, with role-based access control, multi-signature support, and complete transaction audit trails. The critical tension for scenarios: blockchain consensus adds seconds-to-minutes for transaction confirmation, fundamentally incompatible with sub-millisecond traffic decisions. **Audit logging must be asynchronous to real-time control** — a design constraint that creates narrative possibilities around what happens when the audit trail falls behind the decisions it's recording.

**ARM TrustZone** divides processors into Secure and Non-Secure Worlds at the hardware level, deployed on **billions of devices** worldwide. Every bus transaction is tagged with a security bit. For The Edge Negotiator, TrustZone ensures only verified firmware executes on traffic nodes and protects the cryptographic keys used for inter-node negotiation and audit trail signing.

### Real-world deployments and their shadow sides

**Google's Project Green Light** operates across **70+ intersections in 17–18 cities**, processing **30 million car rides monthly** and achieving **30% reduction in vehicle stops**. Yet Seattle DOT had to **reverse a Google-recommended change** because it "did not result in a net benefit." **Alibaba's City Brain** in Hangzhou dropped the city from **5th most congested to 57th** in China's rankings — but relies on government-backed surveillance infrastructure raising transparency concerns. **Siemens SCOOT** manages London's 6,000 intersections and Miami-Dade's 2,900.

The ethical shadow is substantial. San Diego's "smart streetlight" cameras, deployed for traffic analysis, were **repurposed for policing**. AI systems trained on historical data may **prioritize wealthier neighborhoods** for maintenance. Amsterdam and Helsinki have established **AI registries** listing all municipal algorithms with their purpose, data sources, and oversight mechanisms — a real-world precedent for speculative Codes of Conduct.

**Adversarial attacks** are evolving rapidly. The **SLAP** attack uses light projectors to misclassify stop signs with **up to 99% success rate**. The **Acero** framework discovers adversarial driving maneuvers that look innocent but force victim vehicles to violate safety rules, causing collisions in **73.3% of cases**. Dynamic adversarial patches displayed on screens mounted on moving vehicles represent a new class of stealthy, non-co-located attacks. These vulnerabilities provide material for scenarios exploring what happens when The Edge Negotiator is attacked — and what a Code of Conduct requires as response.

## How to write the academic framing

UCL coursework in speculative design requires a **dual-register** approach: creative narrative and analytical rigour must coexist, clearly differentiated but tightly integrated. The literature and UCL's own emphasis on combining "academic rigour with practical skills" at the intersection of "engineering, behavioural sciences, and design" points to a specific structural approach.

### The separated narrative-and-analysis structure

The most widely used and safest approach for coursework: present each speculative scenario as a distinct, visually differentiated section (italicized, boxed, or indented), followed immediately by a "Context and Explanation" section in standard academic prose. The narrative reads as fiction; the analysis maps every fictional element back to theory.

Transitional language between narrative and analysis should be precise and explicit. Use phrases like: "This scenario illustrates what Meadows (1999) identifies as intervention at the level of *information flows*" or "The fictional Code's requirement for mandatory algorithmic disclosure operates at leverage point 6" or "By positioning this scenario in the *plausible* cone, we explore how current edge AI capabilities could evolve under a governance framework that prioritizes..."

James Auger's concept of the **"perceptual bridge"** (2013) provides the key quality criterion: every creative choice should be traceable to either an extrapolation of a real trend, a theoretical concept being explored, or a deliberate provocation rooted in critical theory. The bridge connects the audience's everyday reality to the speculative world — too long a bridge and credibility collapses; too short and the scenario fails to provoke.

### Assessment criteria to target

Based on synthesis across UK design programs and the speculative design literature, markers evaluate four dimensions. **Theoretical grounding** (25–30%): clear articulation of frameworks, accurate citation, and evidence that creative choices are informed by theory. **Quality of speculation** (20–25%): plausibility calibration, criticality (challenging assumptions rather than merely illustrating them), and concrete specificity. **Critical analysis** (25–30%): explicit connections between fictional elements and systemic concepts, identification of feedback dynamics, and honest reflection on what the speculation reveals and obscures. **Communication and craft** (15–20%): writing quality in both registers, effective use of format, and proper referencing.

To demonstrate strong systems thinking, scenarios should **show interconnections** (how changes cascade), **identify feedback loops** (reinforcing and balancing), **address emergence** (behaviors not predictable from components alone), and **engage with leverage points** (showing that some interventions are more powerful than others). The most sophisticated submissions will show awareness that design is political — attending to who benefits, who is harmed, and who decides within each fictional governance framework.

### Mapping the six scenarios

A strong structure for the coursework maps one scenario to each cone position, with each targeting a different leverage point level:

| Scenario | Future Cone | Leverage level | Core dynamic |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Incremental optimization | Probable | Parameters (#12) | Adjusting signal timing thresholds within existing governance |
| 2. Transparent negotiation | Plausible | Information flows (#6) | Mandatory disclosure of edge node decision rationale |
| 3. Emergent green waves | Plausible/Preferable | Self-organization (#4) | System evolving its own traffic patterns beyond designer intent |
| 4. Cascading failure | Possible (unpreferable) | Rules (#5) | Adversarial attack exploits protocol vulnerability, triggering cascade |
| 5. Equity-first mobility | Preferable | Goals (#3) | Optimizing for equitable access rather than throughput |
| 6. Paradigm dissolution | Preposterous boundary | Paradigm (#2) | The system transcends its own Code of Conduct, questioning its optimization target |

Each scenario's Context and Explanation section should name the leverage point, trace the feedback loops depicted, position the scenario within the cone with explicit justification, and connect to at least one real-world precedent (Project Green Light, City Brain, TrustZone deployment, adversarial attack research).

## Conclusion

The strongest approach to this coursework treats the Code of Conduct not as a regulatory document but as a **diegetic prototype** — a material artifact from a fictional world that reveals systemic assumptions through its clauses, definitions, and enforcement mechanisms. Each scenario should feel mundane enough to be mistaken for near-future journalism, yet strange enough to provoke genuine reflection on how decentralized autonomous systems reshape urban life, equity, and governance.

The deepest insight from this research is that **the highest-leverage interventions** in autonomous traffic systems are not technical parameters but questions of goals, paradigms, and self-organization — precisely the territory where speculative design operates best. A Code of Conduct that merely adjusts sensor thresholds operates at leverage point 12; one that questions what "optimal traffic" means for whom, and whether a decentralized system should be permitted to evolve its own answer, operates at leverage points 2 and 3. The progression across Future Cone categories naturally mirrors this escalation from parameter-tweaking to paradigm-challenging — and that structural alignment between Meadows and Dunne & Raby is the coursework's most powerful analytical move.
