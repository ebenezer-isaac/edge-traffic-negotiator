# Professional codes and real-world attacks: an IoT infrastructure ethics analysis

Autonomous IoT traffic systems — where each intersection runs its own SLM agent in a decentralised mesh — expose fundamental gaps in every major professional code of conduct. **None of the three codes analysed below were designed for systems where no single developer, operator, or organisation controls behaviour**, yet all three contain principles that apply with unexpected force to exactly this scenario. Part A maps the precise clauses; Part B grounds the analysis in verified, citable incidents where traffic and transport AI was attacked, fooled, or caused unintended harm.

---

## Part A: Clause-level analysis of professional codes of conduct

### 1. BCS Code of Conduct — where "relevant authority" dissolves

The BCS Code of Conduct (Version 8, approved by Trustee Board 8 June 2022) contains **21 clauses across four sections**. The full text is available at bcs.org/media/2211/bcs-code-of-conduct.pdf.

**Section 1 — Public Interest** contains the clauses most directly relevant to safety-critical IoT:

- **Clause 1a**: *"have due regard for public health, privacy, security and wellbeing of others and the environment."* This is the broadest safety mandate. In a decentralised traffic mesh, every developer contributing an SLM agent module bears this obligation — but the clause assumes a human professional exercising judgment, not an autonomous agent acting on learned patterns. The BCS guidance notes clarify that "acting in the public interest may be governed by Legislation," which creates a problem for emergent behaviour that no individual developer foresaw.

- **Clause 1b**: *"have due regard for the legitimate rights of Third Parties."* The guidance defines Third Parties as *"any person or organisation that might be affected by your activities in your professional capacity, irrespective of whether they are directly aware or involved in those activities."* For a mesh of autonomous intersection agents, **every road user, pedestrian, and nearby resident is a third party** — yet no single BCS member "controls" the aggregate system behaviour.

- **Clause 1d**: *"promote equal access to the benefits of IT and seek to promote the inclusion of all sectors in society wherever opportunities arise."* This mandates equitable service from traffic AI — an obligation that could be violated if autonomous agents learn to optimise throughput on affluent corridors while deprioritising others.

**Section 2 — Professional Competence and Integrity** contains the accountability trap:

- **Clause 2a**: *"only undertake to do work or provide a service that is within your professional competence."* When an SLM agent exhibits emergent behaviour from mesh interactions, **no individual developer can credibly claim competence over the aggregate system**. The guidance notes add: *"Where you are leading a first of kind project, you will ensure that you make use of peer review and support where appropriate"* — yet peer review of emergent multi-agent behaviour remains an unsolved problem.

- **Clause 2c**: *"develop your professional knowledge, skills and competence on a continuing basis, maintaining awareness of technological developments, procedures, and standards that are relevant to your field."* This imposes a living obligation to understand adversarial ML threats to deployed systems.

- **Clause 2f**: *"avoid injuring others, their property, reputation, or employment by false or malicious or negligent action or inaction."* The word **"negligent inaction"** is critical — failing to test for adversarial vulnerabilities in a deployed traffic SLM could constitute negligent inaction under this clause.

**Section 3 — Duty to Relevant Authority** is where the code structurally breaks down for decentralised systems:

- **Clause 3a**: *"carry out your professional responsibilities with due care and diligence in accordance with the Relevant Authority's requirements whilst exercising your professional judgement at all times."* The BCS defines "Relevant Authority" as *"the person(s) or organisation(s) which has/have authority over the activity of individuals in their professional capacity."* In a mesh of autonomous intersection agents spanning multiple local authorities, contracted from multiple vendors, with open-source SLM components maintained by distributed contributors, **there may be no single Relevant Authority**. The guidance states that when conflict arises between the Relevant Authority's instructions and professional judgment, *"you will indicate the likely risks and consequences"* — but to whom, when the system crosses organisational boundaries?

- **Clause 3c**: *"accept professional responsibility for your work and for the work of colleagues who are defined in a given context as working under your supervision."* This clause presupposes hierarchical human supervision. **Autonomous agents are not "colleagues under supervision"** in any sense the BCS drafters intended.

- **Clause 3e**: *"NOT misrepresent or withhold information on the performance of products, systems or services."* This mandates transparency about system capabilities and limitations — directly relevant to disclosing adversarial vulnerability of deployed traffic SLMs.

**Section 4 — Duty to the Profession** reinforces whistleblowing obligations:

- **Clause 4a**: *"accept your personal duty to uphold the reputation of the profession and not take any action which could bring the profession into disrepute."* The guidance notes add an obligation to *"Support a colleague or any other person to whom they have a duty of care who in good faith raises any concern about a danger, risk, malpractice or wrongdoing which affects others (blows the whistle)."*

**Key structural failure**: The BCS Code assumes a **bilateral relationship** (professional ↔ relevant authority) with clear lines of accountability. A decentralised AI traffic mesh creates **n-lateral relationships** where responsibility diffuses across dozens of organisations. The code provides no mechanism for attributing accountability when emergent behaviour from agent interactions causes harm — a problem that the Cruise pedestrian-dragging incident (see Part B) made concrete.

### 2. ACM Code of Ethics (2018) — emergent properties explicitly addressed

The ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct, adopted by ACM Council on 22 June 2018, is the only major code that **explicitly anticipates emergent system behaviour and data aggregation risks**. Full text at acm.org/code-of-ethics.

**Principle 1.1 — "Contribute to society and to human well-being, acknowledging that all people are stakeholders in computing."** The full guidance states that computing professionals have an obligation to use their skills for the benefit of society, promoting fundamental human rights and protecting individuals' right to autonomy. It specifies that *"an essential aim is to minimize negative consequences of computing, including threats to health, safety, personal security, and privacy."* When interests of multiple groups conflict, **those less advantaged should receive increased attention and priority**. For autonomous traffic systems, this creates an explicit ethical obligation to consider pedestrians, cyclists, and residents of disadvantaged areas — not just vehicle throughput.

**Principle 1.2 — "Avoid harm."** This is the most directly relevant principle for IoT infrastructure. The exact text includes: *"Well-intended actions, including those that accomplish assigned duties, may lead to harm. When that harm is unintended, those responsible are obligated to undo or mitigate the harm as much as possible."* The critical passage reads: *"Additionally, the consequences of data aggregation and emergent properties of systems should be carefully analyzed. Those involved with pervasive or infrastructure systems should also consider Principle 3.7."* The principle further states: *"A computing professional has an additional obligation to report any signs of system risks that might result in harm. If leaders do not act to curtail or mitigate such risks, it may be necessary to 'blow the whistle' to reduce potential harm."* The explicit mention of **"emergent properties"** makes this the single most relevant clause for a mesh of autonomous intersection agents, where behaviour emerges from interactions rather than individual design.

**Principle 1.6 — "Respect privacy."** The guidance notes that *"technology enables the collection, monitoring, and exchange of personal information quickly, inexpensively, and often without the knowledge of those affected."* It requires that **only the minimum necessary personal information be collected**, that individuals be able to review and correct their data, and crucially: *"Merging data collections can compromise privacy features present in the original collections. Therefore, computing professionals should take special care for privacy when merging data collections."* For traffic sensors that aggregate vehicle trajectories, pedestrian movements, and environmental data, this creates a direct obligation to treat the **mesh's aggregated data as more privacy-sensitive than any individual sensor's output**.

**Principle 2.5 — "Give comprehensive and thorough evaluations of computer systems and their impacts, including analysis of possible risks."** The guidance contains language specifically addressing AI: *"Extraordinary care should be taken to identify and mitigate potential risks in machine learning systems."* It further states that *"a system for which future risks cannot be reliably predicted requires frequent reassessment of risk as the system evolves in use, or it should not be deployed."* This creates a stark binary for autonomous intersection agents: either implement continuous risk reassessment, **or do not deploy the system**. There is no middle ground in the ACM's framing.

**Principle 2.9 — "Design and implement systems that are robustly and usably secure."** The guidance requires *"due diligence to ensure that the system functions as intended, and appropriate action to secure resources against accidental and intentional misuse, modification, and denial of service."* Post-deployment, professionals must *"integrate mitigation techniques and policies such as monitoring, patching, and vulnerability reporting"* and provide *"timely and clear notification to parties affected by data breaches."* For IoT traffic infrastructure, this mandates defence against the specific adversarial attacks documented in Part B.

**Principle 3.1 — "Ensure that the public good is the central concern during all professional computing work."** This leadership principle requires that organisations establish *"procedures and attitudes oriented toward quality, transparency, and the welfare of society."* For traffic infrastructure, this means transparency obligations fall not only on individual engineers but on the **organisational leaders** commissioning autonomous systems.

**Principle 3.7 — "Recognize and take special care of systems that become integrated into the infrastructure of society."** This is the capstone principle for IoT traffic systems. The guidance states: *"When organizations and groups develop systems that become an important part of the infrastructure of society, their leaders have an added responsibility to be good stewards of these systems."* Stewardship requires *"establishing policies for fair system access, including for those who may have been excluded"* and *"continual monitoring of how society uses a system."* Most critically: *"When appropriate standards of care do not exist, computing professionals have a duty to ensure they are developed."* Since **no adequate standard of care exists for decentralised autonomous traffic AI meshes**, the ACM Code places an affirmative obligation on practitioners to create one.

**Key strength**: Unlike the BCS Code, the ACM explicitly addresses emergent properties, data aggregation, infrastructure responsibility, and ML-specific risk — making it substantially more applicable to autonomous IoT systems. **Key weakness**: Like all professional codes, it assigns obligations to individual "computing professionals" who may have little agency over a system's aggregate behaviour.

### 3. IEEE Ethically Aligned Design (EAD1e, 2019) — the most comprehensive framework

The IEEE EAD1e ("Ethically Aligned Design: A Vision for Prioritizing Human Well-being with Autonomous and Intelligent Systems, First Edition") was published 25 March 2019 under Creative Commons CC BY-NC 4.0. It establishes **eight General Principles**, four of which are most relevant.

**Principle 2 — Well-being**: *"A/IS creators shall adopt increased human well-being as a primary success criterion for development."* The background text explicitly warns that *"A/IS technologies can be narrowly conceived from an ethical standpoint. They can be legal, profitable, and safe in their usage, yet not positively contribute to human and environmental well-being."* The recommendation states: *"A/IS should prioritize human well-being as an outcome in all system designs, using the best available and widely accepted well-being metrics as their reference point."* For traffic AI, this means **optimising for throughput, emissions reduction, or journey time is insufficient** — the system must demonstrate measurable improvement in broader well-being indicators.

**Principle 6 — Accountability**: *"A/IS shall be created and operated to provide an unambiguous rationale for all decisions made."* The background text states that *"it should, if necessary, be possible to apportion culpability among responsible creators (designers and manufacturers) and operators to avoid confusion or fear within the general public."* It explicitly links accountability to transparency: *"Accountability and partial accountability are not possible without transparency."* The recommendations include: *"Systems for registration and record-keeping should be established so that it is always possible to find out who is legally responsible for a particular A/IS."* Parameters to register include **training data, sensors, algorithms, optimisation goals, and loss/reward functions**. For a decentralised traffic mesh, this creates a requirement to maintain a complete provenance record for every intersection agent — a significant technical and governance challenge.

**Principle 5 — Transparency**: *"The basis of a particular A/IS decision should always be discoverable."* The EAD1e identifies **five stakeholder groups** requiring different levels of transparency: users ("what the system is doing and why"), creators/validators ("processes and input data"), accident investigators (who need "secure storage of sensor and internal state data comparable to a flight data recorder"), legal professionals, and the general public. The recommendation to *"develop new standards that describe measurable, testable levels of transparency"* led directly to IEEE 7001. A suggested mechanism is a **"why-did-you-do-that button"** — for traffic infrastructure, this translates to a requirement for intersection agents to log and explain every signal-timing decision.

**Principle 7 — Awareness of Misuse**: *"A/IS creators shall guard against all potential misuses and risks of A/IS in operation."* The background text explicitly references autonomous vehicle hacking: *"Cases of A/IS hacking have already been widely reported, with driverless cars, for example."* It recommends that *"creators should be aware of methods of misuse, and they should design A/IS in ways to minimize the opportunity for these."* This creates a direct obligation to design traffic SLMs with adversarial robustness in mind — not as an afterthought.

**Relevant IEEE P7000 series standards** provide implementation pathways:

- **IEEE 7000-2021** (Model Process for Addressing Ethical Concerns During System Design) — Published, active. Provides a Value-based Engineering (VbE) methodology for embedding ethical values at inception. Directly applicable to commissioning autonomous traffic systems.

- **IEEE 7001-2021** (Transparency of Autonomous Systems) — Published, active. Sponsored by the IEEE Vehicular Technology / Intelligent Transportation Systems committee. Defines **measurable, testable levels of transparency** for five stakeholder groups. Requires "black box" data recording comparable to flight data recorders. This is the most directly applicable standard for autonomous traffic infrastructure.

- **IEEE 7010-2020** (Assessing Impact of A/IS on Human Well-Being) — Published, active. Provides a Well-being Impact Assessment (WIA) framework with 12 well-being domains. **Includes an autonomous vehicles use case** with a sample well-being indicators dashboard. Enables municipalities to measure whether AI traffic management actually improves citizen well-being.

- **IEEE 7012-2025** (Machine Readable Personal Privacy Terms) — Published late 2025. Reverses the privacy consent model: individuals proffer their own machine-readable privacy terms to service providers. Scope explicitly covers "AI agents," making it applicable to autonomous traffic sensors collecting movement data.

**Key structural advantage**: The IEEE framework treats ethics as an **engineering requirement** with measurable, testable properties — not merely aspirational guidance. The P7000 series provides concrete implementation standards that the BCS and ACM codes lack.

## Part B: Documented real-world adversarial attacks on traffic and transport infrastructure

### San Diego's $30 million streetlight surveillance debacle

San Diego deployed approximately **3,300 GE Current "CityIQ" smart streetlights** beginning in 2017, at a cost of **$30.23 million**. Each streetlight contained Intel Atom processors, two 1080p cameras, acoustic sensors, and environmental sensors. The system was publicly pitched as a sustainability initiative for LED upgrades and transportation planning.

In **August 2018**, an SDPD officer investigating a Gaslamp Quarter murder noticed a streetlight camera with a view of the crime scene. GE Current extracted the footage — the first law enforcement use. Deputy COO **Erik Caldwell** later admitted: *"We could have done a better job of communicating with the public that there had been a change in the use of the data."* By February 2019, SDPD had gained direct visual access to the streetlight network via Genetec Clearance digital evidence management software, allowing officers to extract video without multi-step authorisation.

Community backlash was organised through the **TRUST SD Coalition** (30+ organisations), led by **Geneviéve Jones-Wright**. In January 2020, the Public Safety Committee **unanimously rejected** a proposed data-use policy and instead pursued comprehensive surveillance oversight. In **September 2020**, Mayor **Kevin Faulconer** ordered all 3,000+ cameras shut off pending a surveillance ordinance. The **TRUST Surveillance Oversight Ordinance** received final unanimous City Council approval on **2 August 2022**, championed by Council Member **Monica Montgomery Steppe**. The ordinance requires community engagement, Privacy Advisory Board review, and Council approval for all surveillance technology. In 2023, the Council controversially voted 7-2 to restore 500 streetlights with Flock Safety ALPRs ($12 million, 5-year contract), **overriding the Privacy Advisory Board's recommendation to reject the proposal**.

This incident directly illustrates violations of BCS Clause 1a (public health, privacy, security, wellbeing), ACM Principle 1.6 (respect privacy — data collected for one purpose used for another without consent), and ACM Principle 3.7 (infrastructure stewardship). The San Diego County Grand Jury issued a report on the programme in June 2022.

### Google Project Green Light and the limits of single-variable optimisation

Google's **Project Green Light** uses AI and anonymised Google Maps driving data to recommend traffic signal timing adjustments. **Seattle was the first North American city** to partner with Google, beginning in late 2022. Five intersections in Ballard and Greenwood received timing adjustments in 2023.

SDOT **reversed at least one specific Google-recommended timing change** because, per SDOT spokesperson **Mariam Ali** (quoted in *Scientific American*, 15 August 2024), it *"improved congestion for one direction of travel but did not have a benefit to overall intersection/corridor operations."* In Manchester, England, traffic engineers **"often opted to ignore Google's recommendations"** because they had intentionally set stoplights to prioritise bus routes or discourage commuters from driving through residential areas — considerations Google's algorithm did not account for.

Expert criticism centres on **single-variable optimisation**: Google only optimises for fewer personal vehicle stops. Research product manager **Matheus Vervloet** confirmed test intersections were selected specifically to avoid bus lanes, bike lanes, trolley cars, and high-use pedestrian crossings. University of Pittsburgh professor **Aleksandar Stevanovic** warned that prioritising car traffic over transit/bikes/pedestrians may increase driving and emissions through induced demand, noting only **~2% of US transportation emissions come from congestion** (per a 2022 Congressional Budget Office report). Google **declined to answer** *Scientific American*'s questions about financial investment, staffing, or future pricing plans.

This case demonstrates ACM Principle 2.5's requirement that ML systems undergo "frequent reassessment of risk as the system evolves" and IEEE EAD1e's Well-being principle — optimising a single metric does not constitute well-being improvement.

### Adversarial attacks that fooled real autonomous vehicles

Three research teams demonstrated physical adversarial attacks against production Tesla vehicles:

**Tencent Keen Security Lab (March 2019)** placed three small stickers on a road surface at an intersection, mimicking merge striping. A Tesla Model S on Autopilot followed the fake lane markings and **swerved into the oncoming traffic lane**. The researchers also used physical adversarial images to trigger the rain-sensing neural network, activating windshield wipers in dry conditions. Tesla responded that *"a driver can easily override Autopilot at any time."*

**McAfee Advanced Threat Research (February 2020)** placed a **2-inch piece of black electrical tape** horizontally on a 35 mph speed limit sign, extending the numeral "3" to resemble "85." The MobilEye EyeQ3 camera on Tesla's 2016 Model X misread the sign as 85 mph. When Traffic-Aware Cruise Control was engaged, the vehicle began autonomously accelerating toward 85 mph. The driver manually braked at ~50 mph. Only older Tesla vehicles with MobilEye EyeQ3 were affected.

**Ben-Gurion University of the Negev (ACM CCS 2020, DOI: 10.1145/3372297.3423359)** demonstrated "phantom attacks": a phantom stop sign image lasting just **125–500 milliseconds** embedded in a digital billboard advertisement caused Tesla Autopilot to brake suddenly. A ~$300 projector mounted on a drone projected phantom pedestrians onto road surfaces, causing emergency braking. These attacks leave **no physical evidence** and are imperceptible to human drivers.

### Cruise robotaxi: from pedestrian dragging to federal criminal charges

On **2 October 2023**, at Market and Fifth Streets in San Francisco, a hit-and-run vehicle struck a pedestrian crossing against a red light, flinging her into the path of a Cruise robotaxi. The Cruise vehicle braked but ran over the woman. Its detection system **failed to detect the pedestrian trapped underneath**, then executed a pullover manoeuvre that **dragged her approximately 20 feet at up to 7 mph**. She suffered life-threatening injuries but survived.

The consequences cascaded: the California DMV **suspended Cruise's permits** on 24 October 2023; the CPUC suspended passenger operations; **NHTSA fined Cruise $1.5 million** for failing to fully report the crash; the **US Attorney's Office charged Cruise with filing a false report**, resulting in a $500,000 criminal fine under a deferred prosecution agreement (November 2024). CEO Kyle Vogt resigned, GM cut Cruise's annual budget by **$1 billion**, and the company halted all driverless operations nationally. Earlier, on 17 August 2023, a Cruise robotaxi had **failed to yield to a San Francisco fire truck** responding with lights and sirens, injuring a passenger. The SFFD logged **55+ incidents** of autonomous vehicles interfering with emergency operations from May 2022 to August 2023.

### Foundational adversarial attack research on traffic infrastructure

**RP2 — Robust Physical Perturbations (CVPR 2018, DOI: 10.1109/CVPR.2018.00175)**: Kevin Eykholt et al. (University of Michigan, University of Washington, UC Berkeley) demonstrated that carefully computed black-and-white stickers resembling graffiti on real stop signs achieved **100% targeted misclassification in lab tests** and **84.8% in drive-by field tests** from a moving vehicle. The stop sign was misclassified as "Speed Limit 45." This is the foundational paper establishing that physically realisable adversarial perturbations can reliably fool traffic sign classifiers in real-world conditions.

**SLAP — Short-Lived Adversarial Perturbations (USENIX Security 2021)**: Giulio Lovisotto et al. (University of Oxford) used an RGB projector to project adversarial perturbations onto real stop signs, achieving **up to 98–99% misclassification** against YOLOv3 and Mask-RCNN. Unlike sticker attacks, SLAP projections can be **dynamically toggled, leave no physical trace**, and bypassed the SentiNet adversarial-example detector over 95% of the time. The attack is limited by bright daylight conditions.

**"Green Lights Forever" — Traffic Signal Hacking (USENIX WOOT 2014)**: Branden Ghena and J. Alex Halderman (University of Michigan) gained control of a **networked traffic signal system of nearly 100 intersections** with authorisation from a Michigan road agency. They exploited three vulnerabilities: unencrypted 5.8 GHz wireless communications, factory-default credentials published online, and an accessible VxWorks debug port. From a single laptop, they could set all lights to red, alter timing across intersections, and trigger malfunction management units. The traffic controller vendor stated: *"the company has followed the accepted industry standard and it is that standard which does not include security."* Hardware failsafes (MMUs) prevented conflicting greens but could not prevent denial-of-service attacks. **Over 40 US states use similar systems.**

### Physical attacks and interference with autonomous vehicle fleets

Beyond adversarial ML, Waymo vehicles in **Chandler, Arizona** faced **21 documented incidents** of harassment (2017–2018), including Roy Leonard Haselton (age 69) aiming a .22 caliber revolver at a Waymo vehicle and driver (arrested August 2018), and Erik O'Polka running Waymo vehicles off the road with his Jeep on at least six occasions. In **February 2024**, a crowd in San Francisco's Chinatown smashed a Waymo vehicle's windows and **set it on fire** with a firework during Lunar New Year celebrations. In **July 2023**, activist group Safe Street Rebel discovered that placing a standard orange traffic cone on a robotaxi's hood would immobilise it, organising a viral "Week of Cone" campaign ahead of the CPUC expansion hearing.

### GPS and LiDAR spoofing against transportation systems

Real-world GPS spoofing has moved from theoretical to operational. In **June 2013**, Todd Humphreys (University of Texas at Austin) spoofed the GPS of the **$80 million yacht "White Rose of Drachs"** using a briefcase-sized device (~$2,000 in parts), coercing the vessel hundreds of metres off course with **no alarms triggered** (published in *NAVIGATION*, DOI: 10.1002/navi.183). In **2017**, approximately 20 ships in the Black Sea experienced GPS anomalies attributed to Russian spoofing. In **2019**, the British oil tanker *Stena Impero* was reportedly directed into Iranian territorial waters via GPS spoofing before being seized. By **2023**, OpsGroup collected 20+ reports of aircraft GPS spoofing near the Iranian border, including complete inertial reference system corruption.

LiDAR spoofing research has achieved real-world viability. Cao et al. (ACM CCS 2019, DOI: 10.1145/3319535.3339815) demonstrated semantic LiDAR attacks against Baidu Apollo, spoofing fake vehicle obstacles with ~75% success rate. Takami et al. (NDSS 2025) achieved the **first demonstrated LiDAR spoofing attack against a vehicle driving at 60 km/h from 110 metres**, with ≥96% object-removal success rate against a vehicle running Autoware.

## Conclusion: codes written for humans applied to autonomous meshes

Three structural failures emerge from mapping professional codes onto decentralised AI traffic infrastructure. First, **accountability dissolves**: the BCS's "Relevant Authority" concept, the ACM's "computing professional" framing, and even IEEE EAD1e's "creators and operators" distinction all presuppose identifiable human agents — yet a mesh of autonomous SLM intersection controllers creates emergent behaviour that no individual designed or authorised. Second, **transparency requirements exist but lack implementation mechanisms**: IEEE 7001's measurable transparency levels and the EAD1e's "black box" recommendation provide the closest thing to actionable standards, but no deployed traffic system meets them. Third, **the real-world attack surface is broader than any code anticipates**: from RP2 sticker attacks fooling sign classifiers at 84.8% in drive-by tests, to SLAP projections that leave no physical evidence, to the University of Michigan's demonstration that 100 traffic intersections could be compromised from a single laptop, the adversarial threat landscape exceeds what "follow generally accepted best practices" (ACM 1.2) can address.

The ACM Code's explicit mention of "emergent properties of systems" and "data aggregation" makes it the most forward-looking, but its Principle 2.5 creates a stark obligation: a system whose future risks cannot be reliably predicted **must undergo frequent reassessment or must not be deployed**. For a decentralised SLM traffic mesh where each agent learns and adapts independently, this may amount to an ethical prohibition on deployment until continuous monitoring infrastructure — of the kind IEEE 7001 and 7010 describe — is in place. The San Diego streetlight case, the Cruise pedestrian dragging, and the Green Lights Forever traffic signal hack are not hypotheticals; they are the documented precedents that make this analysis urgent.
