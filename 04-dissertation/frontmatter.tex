\begin{abstract}
When a signalised junction is involved in a collision, someone must prove
what the controller knew and what it decided. Published language-model
controllers are cloud-served and cannot answer that question. This
report asks whether a cabinet-sized language model can control real London
junctions competently and bind every actuation into a record an
investigator can trust, and what fine-tuning contributes. The answer,
inside a deterministic shield, is that what fine-tuning reliably moves is
\emph{authorship}, not delay.

The full stack runs on one consumer device: a small language model
served by Microsoft Foundry Local within 6\,GB proposes phases behind a
MaxPressure shield and anti-starvation floor, and every decision is
Ed25519-signed into a per-junction hash chain, reconstructable after a
collision into a cited UK-law evidence pack. A frontier teacher is distilled
into 0.6B and 3.8B students with QLoRA, compiled to int4 ONNX, and evaluated
offline on a hash-frozen holdout and closed-loop in SUMO on measured London
topologies over thirty training-disjoint seeds.

The model's share of logged decision ticks rises from
0.39--0.62 to 0.56--0.77, while its divergence from MaxPressure among the
decisions it authors falls from 0.37--0.60 to 0.04--0.17, a deflationary
reading I bound. Delay moves only
where the network can clear: on a Bloomsbury grid calibrated so that 87\%
of vehicles complete, the distilled controller beats the fixed-time floor
by 8.8\% where the stock one loses by 11.9\%, an 18.3\% head-to-head gain
on 30 of 30 seeds ($p{=}2.0{\times}10^{-21}$); the same calibration on the
Euston corridor does not reproduce it. Distillation saturates at 0.6B, a
three-seed MaxPressure result is retracted at thirty seeds, seven collision
scenarios reconstruct with tampering caught, and a trust ledger catches
blatant but not calibrated stealth misreporting.
\end{abstract}
