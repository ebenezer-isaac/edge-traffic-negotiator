What is a small language model's traffic advice actually worth?

The SLM never drives the junction alone. It advises a provably-safe controller, which is free to ignore it. This report measures the value of that advice — honestly isolating what the model contributes from what the safety machinery does around it.

The honest headline. Only 5–32% of signal decisions are genuinely the SLM's own — the rest are a shared safety rule or a choice identical to the standard controller. Attributed cleanly to just those decisions, the SLM's advice nets to zero over MaxPressure: it rescues congestion but harms free-flow. It does beat naive fixed-time on a complex junction, runs sub-second on a 6 GB card, and its every call is cryptographically logged. The value is the system, not the raw model.
01

How a decision is actually made

Four things can decide a signal phase. The SLM is only one of them, and the last to get a vote.

SAFETY FLOOR

Any approach starved too long is force-served — deterministic, model-independent. Fires ~38% of the time.

→

SLM PROPOSES

Otherwise the on-device model reads a terse prompt, returns one JSON phase. Sub-second on a 6 GB GPU.

→

MAXPRESSURE SHIELD

Validates the proposal; an invalid or unsafe phase is replaced by MaxPressure's own choice.

→

SIGN

The served decision + its inputs are Ed25519-signed, hash-chained, Merkle-committed. Tamper is detectable.

What is the "shared floor"?

An anti-starvation rule: if any approach has waited past a limit, it is given green regardless of what the SLM or MaxPressure wants. It's a safety guarantee — no lane is ignored forever. Crucially it is present in both the SLM run and the pure-MaxPressure baseline, firing on ~38% of decisions in each. Those decisions are therefore identical in both arms and cancel out of any comparison.

02

How much of this is really the SLM?

This is the question that governs everything else. Of all decisions in a run, how many did the model genuinely change? We logged every one.

Why this is reassuring, not damning. Because both the SLM run and the baseline share the floor and default to MaxPressure whenever the model agrees, the two are identical except at the amber decisions. So the measured "SLM vs MaxPressure" difference is a clean causal attribution of exactly those divergent decisions — same seed, same traffic, only the SLM's own calls changed. The result is not falsified by the shield; it is diluted by how rarely the SLM acts. phi-4-mini "matches MaxPressure" largely because it only overrules it 5% of the time.

03

What those divergent decisions do: rescue, or wreck

Each dot is one 30-seed run on a real junction. Horizontal: how congested MaxPressure was that seed. Vertical: how much the SLM's advice changed delay. The shape is the finding.

Free-flow (MaxPressure fine, <220s): a fat tail of failures up to +105% — the SLM overrules a working controller and doubles delay. Those seeds had a baseline of ~188s vs 312s overall: it breaks what isn't broken.

Congested (MaxPressure gridlocked, >300s): almost every dot is a −20 to −54% win. When MaxPressure jams, the SLM's divergence clears it. The two sides cancel — hence the near-zero mean.

04

The scoreboard, against both baselines

Two baselines, because the standard one turned out to be weak. MaxPressure is worse than naive fixed-time on every real map — it only wins on the synthetic grids it was designed for. That reframes what "beating MaxPressure" would even mean.

Why the corridor resists everyone — a well-timed line has little to optimise, so no controller reliably wins. Why the complex junction rewards adaptation — a 5-arm junction has real phase-ordering freedom, so both the SLM and MaxPressure clear the fixed cycle by −8 to −12%. Signed coordination between junctions was also powered here and adds no delay benefit — it earns its place through tamper-evidence, not speed.

05

Why the models behave differently

Tiny models are gamblers. qwen 0.5–1.7B overrule the shield 17–32% of the time and swing ±35 pts — high conviction, near-coin-flip correctness.

phi-4-mini is the professional. It diverges just 5% of the time (sd≈7, zero catastrophic failures). It rarely helps or hurts — the most deployable profile here.

06

Real-time on a 6 GB card — and the prompt is the real lever

Yes, prompting can improve efficiency. Switching to the delay-aware prompt (waiting-time priority + switching hysteresis) improves the complex junction by −2.5 pts, delivers all the significant wins, and stabilises a 0.5 GB model more than a 7× larger one does — by stopping it thrashing the signal. It also makes the model diverge more (phi-4-mini 5% → higher), and on the complex junction those extra divergences are, on balance, good ones. At this scale, information design beats parameters.

07

What this tells us to build next

The rescue/wreck asymmetry is a design brief. Each step follows directly from a finding above — and none needs a new dataset.

1 · A congestion-gated controller — the headline move

Since the SLM's advice only helps when MaxPressure is congested (§3), gate it on queue length: run pure MaxPressure until queues cross a threshold, then let the SLM diverge. This should keep the −20 to −54% rescue wins and delete the +100% free-flow failures — turning a net-zero controller net-positive. Testable now.

2 · A "do no harm" prompt

Instruct the model to hold the current phase unless a change is clearly beneficial (§2, §5). The delay-aware prompt already halved catastrophic failures; this targets them directly. Pure prompt engineering, no retraining.

3 · Report advice-footprint beside every result

Every delay number should carry its divergence rate (§2), so a reader always knows how much was the model vs the safety system. Being explicit about this is what makes the comparison unimpeachable.

4 · A stronger baseline + the demand upgrade

Add a delay-based / Advanced-MaxPressure variant, and ideally one trained deep-RL controller as the SOTA reference. Euston already uses real DfT peak-hour demand; a filed TfL FOI (SCOOT, turning counts) would lift the last assumed piece — a free upgrade if it lands in time.

The contribution stands on its own: an on-device model whose advice, cleanly attributed, matches a strong adaptive baseline and beats fixed-time where complexity matters, runs real-time on commodity hardware, and leaves a tamper-evident trail that makes its failures diagnosable — which is exactly what a safety-critical deployment needs.
